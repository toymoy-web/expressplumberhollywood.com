Symptoms

How a Failing Sump Pump Shows Itself

A pump in a pit is out of sight, so the clues come from sound and water level.

Runs without stopping

A stuck float, a failed check valve or a pit that refills as fast as it empties.

Hums but does not pump

A jammed impeller, a clogged intake or a failing motor.

Cycles every few seconds

Water is flowing back down the discharge pipe because the check valve is leaking.

Gritty or grinding noise

Sand or shell fragments are wearing the impeller.

Pit full and silent

Power loss, a tripped breaker or a float that will not rise.

Rust or white crust on hardware

Brackish groundwater is attacking metal parts.

Local Context

Why a Pit Pump Makes Sense in a Low, Wet Place

Hollywood sits low, close to the Atlantic and the Intracoastal Waterway, and the water table in South Florida lies only a few feet below the surface. Groundwater here rises and falls with the same rains and tides as the canals around it.

That is why basements are rare and why pumps show up in other places. A garage pit, a crawl space under an older home, a pool equipment pit and a French drain collecting yard runoff are the usual homes for a pump, and a lower-level bath in a few houses uses an ejector.

Because the ground stays wet, a pump here often runs more than a pump in a dry climate. That makes the float, the check valve and the discharge line the parts that fail first.

Code

What the Plumbing Code Expects of a Pit and Its Pump

Plumbing in Florida is governed by the Florida Building Code, Plumbing, which follows the International Plumbing Code (IPC). For ejector pits that receive wastewater, IPC Section 712 calls for a sealed, gas-tight cover, a vent and a check valve placed next to the pump with a shutoff valve beyond it.

A clear-water sump has fewer rules, but the same good practice applies. A cover keeps debris and mosquitoes out, a check valve stops backflow and the discharge should end away from the house. Replacement or new installation of a pit may need a permit, and we point out what your city requires.

Storm Season

Why Backup Power Matters Before Hurricane Season

Tropical storms bring the rain that fills a pit and the outages that stop the pump. The moment you most need it can be the moment it has no power.

A battery backup and a high-water alarm each address that gap. Test the system before the season starts by pouring water into the pit and confirming the pump starts, runs and shuts off.

Around Broward

Septic, Sewer Backups and Pits Often Overlap

A pit that keeps filling can be a sign of a high water table, a leaking supply line or a saturated drain field nearby. About 44 percent of Hollywood is on septic, so a wet yard and a busy pump may point to the same cause.

If sewage is involved, treat it as a sewer problem first. Sewer backup service and leak detection testing help sort out where the water is coming from, and the Pembroke Park page explains how former quarry lakes keep the ground wet there.

Sump Pump Questions

South Florida homes rarely have basements, so a sump pump is more common in garage pits, crawl spaces, pool equipment pits, elevator pits and French drain systems than in a basement corner. If you have a pit with a pump in it, it is there for a reason and worth keeping working.

A sump pump moves clear groundwater or rainwater out of a pit. An ejector pump lifts wastewater from a below-grade toilet, shower or laundry up to the drain line. Ejector basins need a gas-tight cover and a vent, and a failure is a sewage problem instead of a water problem.

A shallow water table can keep a pit refilling, especially after the rainy season and at high tides. A stuck float or a failed check valve also makes a pump cycle constantly. A short test tells which one it is.

In a storm the power often fails when the ground is already full, which is exactly when the primary pump is needed. A battery backup keeps the pit working for a limited time, so it is worth discussing for any pit that protects finished space or equipment.
