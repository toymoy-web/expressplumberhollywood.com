The Problem

What Hardness Does to a Florida House

Hard water carries dissolved calcium and magnesium. When water heats or evaporates, those minerals form scale, which crusts faucets, clogs aerators and coats heating elements. A heater with a layer of scale works harder and fails sooner.

Hollywood's water comes from limestone in the Biscayne Aquifer. The city softens it with lime and ferric chloride at the plant, and the remaining minerals can still show up as white spots on glass and crusty showerheads.

Choices

Salt-Based Softener or Salt-Free Conditioner

A salt-based softener swaps hardness minerals for sodium or potassium and regenerates with a brine solution. It actually removes the minerals, and it needs salt refills and a drain connection.

A salt-free conditioner changes how the minerals behave so they form less scale. It needs no salt and no drain, but it does not remove the minerals, and results vary with the water and the device.

The right answer depends on your hardness, your goals and any HOA or septic constraints. A plumber should explain the pros and cons before quoting.

Troubleshooting

What to Check When a Softener Stops Working

  • 1Look in the brine tank for salt, and break up any crust or bridge you find.
  • 2Check that the unit has power and the display is on.
  • 3Run a manual regeneration cycle if the manual allows.
  • 4Test the water with a hardness test strip before and after the unit.
  • 5If hardness stays high, call for resin, valve or injector service.

Fit With Other Equipment

Where a Softener Fits in the Plumbing

A softener usually goes at the main line after the shutoff, before the water heater, with a bypass so you can isolate it. It needs a drain connection, an air gap on the brine line and a power outlet.

Water that gets softened and then heated lasts longer on heating elements. A softener also protects fixtures and appliances downstream, so owners often add one when installing a new heater or tankless unit. See tankless water heater service and water filtration installation for related equipment.

HOAs, Condos and Septic Homes

Condo owners often cannot add a whole-house softener without board approval, since the main sits in a shared area. Single-family homes in HOAs may need approval for a visible installation.
