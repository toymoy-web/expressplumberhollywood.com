Two Cities in One

Pembroke Pines Has a 1950s Side and a 1990s Side

Pembroke Pines started in 1954 as two small subdivisions between the airport's edge, SW 72nd Avenue and the Florida Turnpike, on land that had been dairy farms. The first homes went up in 1956, the village incorporated in 1959 and the city in January 1960.

In 1980 the city annexed land from Flamingo Road to U.S. 27, which doubled its size, and rapid building followed. Today the city covers about 34 square miles at roughly 8 feet above sea level, and most homes date from the 1990s while thousands of 1960s houses remain, mostly in the older eastern neighborhoods.

For a homeowner, that means the age of the house tells us where to look first. A 1960 ranch and a 1996 two-story share a city and almost nothing else under the floor.

Emergency dispatch for Pembroke Pines runs around the clock, so a garage water heater that fails at 2 a.m. gets the same answer as one that fails at noon. The dispatcher asks the year the house was built before sending anyone, because it narrows the likely cause.

City Utilities

Who Fixes What: The City, an HOA or You

Pembroke Pines operates its own water and sewer departments. A break in the street, low pressure across the neighborhood or a sewer main backup goes to the city. A leak after your meter or a clog in your own lateral is yours.

Homeowners associations add a third layer. Many communities maintain common irrigation, entry features and lakes, and an irrigation line fed from the city supply needs a backflow assembly tested every year.

If you are not sure which pipe is yours, call and describe where the water is. The first visit separates your side from the city's side.

Common Calls

Emergency Calls in the Pines: Water Heaters, Slab Leaks and Sewer Lines

The most common emergency in a typical Pembroke Pines house is a tank water heater failing in the garage. Water heater repair starts with a plain repair-or-replace answer, and a leaking tank body always means replacement.

The second pattern is a bill that climbs for no clear reason. A meter that moves with every tap closed points to a hidden leak, often under the slab. The third is a backup after heavy rain, which is a job for a camera. Repairs follow the International Plumbing Code (IPC) as adopted in the Florida Building Code, Plumbing, and if the lateral is damaged the job is sewer lateral repair.
